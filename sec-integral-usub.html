    Section 4.6 Substitution Rule for Integration

    Objectives

    • Recognize when an integrand has the form \(f(g(x))g'(x)\) and apply substitution.
      🔗
    • Use substitution to evaluate indefinite integrals and then check results by differentiation.
      🔗
    • Use substitution to evaluate definite integrals.
      🔗
    • Use symmetry (even/odd functions) to evaluate certain definite integrals quickly.
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Reversing the Chain Rule

    In this section we develop a powerful method for finding antiderivatives by reversing the Chain Rule:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{d}{dx}\big(f(g(x))\big)=f'(g(x))g'(x) \end{equation*}
    Let’s see an example of this in action before we develop a systematic method for applying it.
    🔗

    Example 4.6.1.

    (a)
    Find the derivative of \(F(x) = \sin(x^2)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    By the Chain Rule, \(F'(x) = \cos(x^2)\cdot 2x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    Use you answer from (a) to help evaluate \(\displaystyle \int x\cos(x^2)\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since \(F'(x) = \cos(x^2)\cdot 2x\text{,}\) we have
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \displaystyle \int x\cos(x^2)\,dx \amp = \displaystyle \int \tfrac{1}{2}(2x)\cos(x^2)\,dx \\ \amp = \tfrac12\sin(x^2)+C \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    We can check this by differentiating: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big(\tfrac12\sin(x^2)+C\big)=\tfrac12\cos(x^2)\cdot 2x=x\cos(x^2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Notice in our last example, the inside function \(g(x)=x^2\) appears inside the cosine, and its derivative is \(g'(x)=2x\) also appeared in the integrand (off by only a constant) so were were able to rewrite it to match the Chain Rule pattern.
    🔗

    Strategy 4.6.2. U-Substitution Technique.

    1. Given an integral \(\int h(x)\,dx\text{.}\) Try to identify an inside function \(g(x)\) so that \(g'(x)\) appears as a factor (possibly after multiplying and dividing by a constant):
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} h(x)\ =\ f(g(x))\cdot g'(x). \end{equation*}
      After factoring out \(g'(x)\text{,}\) rewrite the remaining part as a function of \(u=g(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Using the symbolic notation \(u=g(x)\) and
      \begin{equation*} du = \dfrac{du}{dx} dx = g'(x) dx \end{equation*}
      Then rewrite the integral in \(u\text{:}\)
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \int h(x)\,dx \ =\ \int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx \ =\ \int f(u)\,du. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    3. Compute \(\int f(u)\,du\) using basic antiderivatives and rules.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Substitute back \(u=g(x)\) to express the final answer in terms of \(x\text{:}\)
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \int h(x)\,dx \ =\ F(u)+C \ =\ F(g(x))+C. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    🔗
    Let’s try Example 4.6.1 again this time with the substitution method in mind.
    🔗

    Example 4.6.3.

    Evaluate \(\displaystyle \int x\cos(x^2)\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We want to identify an inside function \(g(x)\) so that \(g'(x)\) appears as a factor in the integrand. The only reasonable choice is \(u=x^2\text{,}\) since it appears inside the cosine and its derivative is a constant multiple of the other factor in the integrand:
    🔗
    So with \(u=x^2\text{,}\) we have \(du=2x\,dx\text{,}\) so \(\tfrac12\,du=x\,dx\text{.}\) Then the integral becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \displaystyle \int x\cos(x^2)\,dx \amp = \displaystyle \int \cos(u) (\tfrac12\,du) \\ \amp = \tfrac12\int \cos(u)\,du \\ \amp = \tfrac12\sin(u)+C \\ \amp = \tfrac12\sin(x^2)+C. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Notice that the substitution method is just a systematic way to reverse the Chain Rule. In the previous example, we identified the inside function and its derivative by inspection, but here we are more methodical. We first identify the inside function and then rewrite the integral in terms of \(u\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let’s get some more practice!
    🔗

    Example 4.6.4.

    Evaluate \(\displaystyle \int \frac{\sec^2(\sqrt{x})}{\sqrt{x}}\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Check your answer by differentiating.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(u=\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}\text{.}\) Then \(du=\tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\,dx=\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\,dx\text{.}\) Rewrite to produce \(du\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rcl} \displaystyle \int \frac{\sec^2(\sqrt{x})}{\sqrt{x}}\,dx &=& \displaystyle \int 2\sec^2(\sqrt{x})\cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\,dx \\[0.9em] &=& \displaystyle \int 2\sec^2(u)\,du \ =\ 2\tan(u)+C \\[0.9em] &=& \displaystyle 2\tan(\sqrt{x})+C. \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Check by differentiating:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \dfrac{d}{dx}\big(2\tan(\sqrt{x})+C\big) \amp = 2\sec^2(\sqrt{x})\cdot \dfrac{d}{dx}(\sqrt{x}) \\ \amp = 2\sec^2(\sqrt{x})\cdot \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \\ \amp = \dfrac{\sec^2(\sqrt{x})}{\sqrt{x}}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    So the derivative of our answer matches the original integrand, confirming that our antiderivative is correct.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We can also evaluate definite integrals with substitution by first evaluating the antiderivative and then applying the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
    🔗

    Example 4.6.5.

    Evaluate \(\displaystyle \int_0^1 \frac{x^2}{x^3+1}\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First let’s drop the limits and focus on finding an antiderivative.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle \int\frac{x^2}{x^3+1}\,dx \end{equation*}
    Using our u-substitution method, let \(u=x^3+1\text{.}\) Then \(du=3x^2\,dx\text{,}\) so \(\tfrac{1}{3}du=x^2\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rcl} \displaystyle \int\frac{x^2}{x^3+1}\,dx &=& \displaystyle \int \frac{1}{3}\cdot \frac{1}{u}\,du \\[0.9em] &=& \displaystyle \frac{1}{3}\ln|u|+C \\[0.9em] &=& \frac{1}{3}\ln|x^3+1|+C. \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Now we can apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to evaluate the original definite integral:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rcl} \displaystyle \int_0^1 \frac{x^2}{x^3+1}\,dx &=& \displaystyle \left.\frac{1}{3}\ln|x^3+1|\right|_{x=0}^{x=1} \\[0.9em] &=& \displaystyle \frac{1}{3}\ln(2)-\frac{1}{3}\ln(1) \ =\ \frac{1}{3}\ln(2). \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Alternatively for definite integrals we could have instead updated the limits of integration to be in terms of \(u\) and then evaluated the resulting integral without first finding an antiderivative in terms of \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 4.6.6.

    Evaluate \(\displaystyle \int_0^1 \frac{x^2}{x^3+1}\,dx\) by substituting and updating the limits of integration.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(u=x^3+1\text{.}\) Then \(du=3x^2\,dx\text{,}\) so \(\tfrac{1}{3}du=x^2\,dx\text{.}\) Update the limits:
    • when \(x=0\text{,}\) \(u=0^3+1=1\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • when \(x=1\text{,}\) \(u=1^3+1=2\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rcl} \displaystyle \int_0^1 \frac{x^2}{x^3+1}\,dx &=& \displaystyle \int_1^2 \frac{1}{3}\cdot \frac{1}{u}\,du \\[0.9em] &=& \displaystyle \left.\frac{1}{3}\ln|u|\right|_{u=1}^{u=2} \\[0.9em] &=& \frac{1}{3}\ln(2)-\frac{1}{3}\ln(1) = \frac{1}{3}\ln(2). \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Notice that we get the same answer as in Example 4.6.5, but we never had to write down an antiderivative in terms of \(x\) or apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus in terms of \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Two Important Results

    Using our substitution method we can find antiderivatives of some important functions that we will need later in Calculus 2 and beyond.
    🔗

    Theorem 4.6.7.

    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle \int \tan(x)\,dx=-\ln|\cos(x)|+C \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    First we should recognize that \(\tan(x)=\dfrac{\sin(x)}{\cos(x)}\text{,}\) so the integral becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle \int \tan(x)\,dx \ =\ \displaystyle \int \frac{\sin(x)}{\cos(x)}\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Let \(u=\cos(x)\text{.}\) Then \(du=-\sin(x)\,dx\text{,}\) so \(\sin(x)\,dx=-du\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rcl} \displaystyle \int \frac{\sin(x)}{\cos(x)}\,dx &=& \displaystyle -\int \frac{1}{u}\,du \\[0.9em] &=& \displaystyle -\ln|u|+C \\[0.9em] &=& -\ln|\cos(x)|+C. \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Theorem 4.6.8.

    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle \int \sec(x)\,dx= \ln|\sec(x)+\tan(x)|+C \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Multiply and divide the integrand by \(\sec(x)+\tan(x)\) so we can rewrite it as:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \displaystyle \int \sec(x)\,dx \amp = \displaystyle \int \frac{\sec(x)(\sec(x)+\tan(x))}{\sec(x)+\tan(x)}\,dx \\ \amp = \displaystyle \int \frac{\sec^2(x)+\sec(x)\tan(x)}{\sec(x)+\tan(x)}\,dx \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Let \(u=\sec(x)+\tan(x)\text{,}\) then \(du=(\sec(x)\tan(x)+\sec^2(x))\,dx\text{.}\) So now we can evaluate:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rcl} \displaystyle \int \frac{\sec^2(x)+\sec(x)\tan(x)}{\sec(x)+\tan(x)}\,dx &=& \displaystyle \int \frac{1}{u}\,du \\[0.9em] &=& \displaystyle \ln|u|+C \\[0.9em] &=& \ln|\sec(x)+\tan(x)|+C. \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Integral Symmetry for Even and Odd Functions

    The last application of substitution we will discuss in this section is using symmetry to evaluate certain definite integrals quickly.
    🔗
    If you need a quick reminder about even and odd functions, we discussed them earlier in the course in the Curve Sketching Section  of the text. The following result provides a powerful tool for evaluating definite integrals of even and odd functions.
    🔗

    Theorem 4.6.9. Integral Symmetry Theorem.

    • If \(f\) is odd, meaning \(f(-x)=-f(x)\text{,}\) then for any \(a>0\text{,}\)
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \int_{-a}^{a} f(x)\,dx \ =\ 0. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    • If \(f\) is even, meaning \(f(-x)=f(x)\text{,}\) then for any \(a>0\text{,}\)
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \int_{-a}^{a} f(x)\,dx \ =\ 2\int_{0}^{a} f(x)\,dx. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Suppose \(f(x)\) is an odd function. Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \int_{-a}^{a} f(x)\,dx \amp = \int_{-a}^{0} f(x)\,dx + \int_{0}^{a} f(x)\,dx \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Let \(u=-x\text{,}\) then \(du=-dx\) and change the bounds of our first integral.
    • When \(x=-a\text{,}\) \(u=a\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • When \(x=0\text{,}\) \(u=0\text{.}\)
      🔗
    So the first integral becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{-a}^{0} f(x)\,dx = \int_{a}^{0} f(-u)(-du) = \int_{0}^{a} f(-u)\,du. \end{equation*}
    Since \(f\) is odd, \(f(-u)=-f(u)\text{,}\) so the first integral becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{0}^{a} f(-u)\,du = \int_{0}^{a} -f(u)\,du = -\int_{0}^{a} f(u)\,du. \end{equation*}
    So now we have
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \int_{-a}^{a} f(x)\,dx \amp = -\int_{0}^{a} f(u)\,du + \int_{0}^{a} f(x)\,dx \\ \amp = 0. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    The proof for even functions is similar, but we use the fact that \(f(-u)=f(u)\) instead of \(f(-u)=-f(u)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \int_{-a}^{a} f(x)\,dx \amp = \int_{-a}^{0} f(x)\,dx + \int_{0}^{a} f(x)\,dx \\ \amp = \int_{0}^{a} f(-u)\,du + \int_{0}^{a} f(x)\,dx \\ \amp = \int_{0}^{a} f(u)\,du + \int_{0}^{a} f(x)\,dx \\ \amp = 2\int_{0}^{a} f(x)\,dx. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    While the result for even functions can speed up the evaluation of definite integrals (since evaluating at \(0\) is often simpler), the result for odd functions can make some otherwise difficult integrals much easier to evaluate.
    🔗

    Example 4.6.10.

    Evaluate \(\displaystyle \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x\cos(x)\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The integrand \(f(x)=x\cos(x)\) is odd because \(f(-x)=(-x)\cos(-x)=(-x)\cos(x)=-f(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By the symmetry theorem,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x\cos(x)\,dx \ =\ 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Practice / Study Problems

    Some True/False Problems.

    Determine whether each statement is true or false and briefly justify your reasoning.
    🔗
    1.
    \(\displaystyle \int \tan(3x) \,dx = -\tfrac{1}{3}\ln|\cos(3x)|+C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    True. Let \(u=3x\text{,}\) then \(du=3\,dx\text{,}\) so \(\tfrac{1}{3}du=dx\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \displaystyle \int \tan(3x) \,dx \amp = \displaystyle \int \tan(u) \cdot \tfrac{1}{3}\,du \\ \amp = -\tfrac{1}{3}\ln|\cos(u)|+C \\ \amp = -\tfrac{1}{3}\ln|\cos(3x)|+C. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    So the statement is true.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    2.
    The substitution \(u=x^2+1\) is useful for evaluating \(\displaystyle \int \frac{x}{x^2+1}\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    True. Let \(u=x^2+1\text{,}\) then \(du=2x\,dx\text{,}\) so \(\tfrac{1}{2}du=x\,dx\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \displaystyle \int \frac{x}{x^2+1}\,dx \amp = \displaystyle \int \frac{1}{2}\cdot \frac{1}{u}\,du \\ \amp = \displaystyle \frac{1}{2}\ln|u|+C \\ \amp = \displaystyle \frac{1}{2}\ln|x^2+1|+C. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    So the substitution is useful for evaluating the integral, and the statement is true.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    3.
    If \(\displaystyle \int f(x)\,dx = F(x)+C\text{,}\) then \(\displaystyle \int f(4x)\,dx = F(4x)+C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    False. Let \(u=4x\text{,}\) then \(du=4\,dx\text{,}\) so \(\tfrac{1}{4}du=dx\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \displaystyle \int f(4x)\,dx \amp = \displaystyle \int f(u) \cdot \tfrac{1}{4}\,du \\ \amp = \tfrac{1}{4}F(u)+C \\ \amp = \tfrac{1}{4}F(4x)+C. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    So the statement is false.

    \(\displaystyle \int_0^1 xe^{x^2} \,dx = e-1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    False. Let \(u=x^2\text{,}\) then \(du=2x\,dx\text{,}\) so \(\tfrac{1}{2}du=x\,dx\text{.}\) Then

    So the statement is false.

    If \(\displaystyle \int_0^1 f(u) \,du = 5\) then \(\displaystyle \int_0^1 xf(x^2) \,dx = 5/2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    True. Let \(u=x^2\text{,}\) then \(du=2x\,dx\text{,}\) so \(\tfrac{1}{2}du=x\,dx\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \displaystyle \int_0^1 xf(x^2) \,dx \amp = \displaystyle \int_0^1 f(u) \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\,du \\ \amp = \displaystyle \frac{1}{2} \int_0^1 f(u) \,du \\ \amp = \displaystyle \frac{1}{2} \cdot 5 \\ \amp = \displaystyle \frac{5}{2}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    So the statement is true.

    7.
    If \(f(x)\) is odd, then \(\displaystyle \int_{-5}^{5} (f(x)+3)\,dx = 30\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    True. Since \(f(x)\) is odd, \(\displaystyle \int_{-5}^{5} f(x)\,dx = 0\text{.}\) So
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \displaystyle \int_{-5}^{5} (f(x)+3)\,dx \amp = \displaystyle \int_{-5}^{5} f(x)\,dx + \int_{-5}^{5} 3\,dx \\ \amp = 0 + 3\cdot (5-(-5)) \\ \amp = 30. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    So the statement is true.
